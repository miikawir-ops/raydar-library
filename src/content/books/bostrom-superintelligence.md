    Nick Bostrom kysyy, mitä tapahtuu, jos ihminen onnistuu luomaan superälyn eli älyn, joka ylittää selvästi ihmisen kognitiiviset kyvyt lähes kaikilla aloilla. Hänen mukaansa kyse on suurimmasta ja vaikeimmasta haasteesta, jonka ihmiskunta on kohdannut. Jos siinä epäonnistutaan, vihamielinen tai välinpitämätön tekoäly voi tuhota ihmiskunnan.

    Kirja alkaa keskeneräisellä tarulla varpusista, jotka päättävät etsiä pöllönmunan ja kasvattaa pöllön palvelijakseen. Vain yksi varpunen ehdottaa, että ensin pitäisi miettiä, miten pöllö kesytetään. Muut lykkäävät kysymyksen, koska munan löytäminen on jo tarpeeksi vaikeaa. Bostrom ei kerro, miten tarina päättyy, mutta omistaa kirjansa varovaiselle varpuselle. Taru tiivistää kirjan kysymyksen: käyttääkö tekoäly, jos se toteutuu, valtavia kykyjään tavalla, jota ihminen ei enää hallitse?

    Kirja etenee kolmessa vaiheessa, kuten sen alaotsikko kertoo. Ensin Bostrom käy läpi tiet superälyyn: tekoälyn, aivojen emuloinnin, ihmisen biologisen älyn parantamisen, aivojen ja tietokoneen liitännät sekä verkostot. Sitten hän erittelee vaarat. Kun ihmistasoinen kone on olemassa, superäly seuraa todennäköisesti yllättävän nopeasti, ja ensimmäinen superäly voi saada ratkaisevan strategisen edun. Lähes mikä tahansa päämäärä voi johtaa siihen, että se hankkii resursseja ja vastustaa sammuttamistaan. Bostrom pitää tätä aikamme olennaisimpana tehtävänä. Hän myöntää, että monet kirjan yksityiskohdat ovat todennäköisesti vääriä, mutta perustelee, että riski on niin suuri, että siihen on varauduttava jo nyt.

    If humans manage to build a machine that surpasses them in intelligence in almost every domain, it could quickly become so powerful that it can no longer be controlled. Its goals must therefore be made beneficial to humanity before it is created: there is only one chance.
  yleiskuva: |
    Nick Bostrom asks what happens if humanity succeeds in creating superintelligence, an intellect that greatly exceeds human cognitive performance in virtually all domains. In his view this is the most important and most daunting challenge humanity has ever faced. If we fail, a malevolent or indifferent artificial intelligence could destroy us.

    The book opens with an unfinished fable about sparrows who decide to find an owl egg and raise the owl as their servant. Only one sparrow suggests they should first work out how to tame an owl. The others put the question off, since finding an egg will be hard enough. Bostrom does not say how the story ends, but dedicates the book to the cautious sparrow. The fable captures the book's question: if AI is achieved, will it use its vast capabilities in ways beyond human control?

    The book proceeds in three steps, as its subtitle says. First Bostrom surveys the paths to superintelligence: artificial intelligence, whole brain emulation, biological enhancement of human intelligence, brain-computer interfaces and networks. Then he analyses the dangers. Once a human-level machine exists, superintelligence would most likely follow surprisingly quickly, and the first superintelligence could gain a decisive strategic advantage. Almost any goal could lead it to acquire resources and resist being switched off. Finally he weighs strategies: capability control, motivation selection and how development should be steered globally.

    The conclusion is that the AI control problem must be solved before the first superintelligence arrives, because afterwards its goals can no longer be changed. Bostrom calls this the essential task of our age. He admits that many of the book's points are probably wrong, but argues that the risk is large enough that we must prepare for it now.

    The book reached the New York Times list of best-selling science books and made existential risk from AI a subject of wide debate. Elon Musk and Bill Gates recommended it, and the philosophers Peter Singer and Derek Parfit received it as a work of importance.
  ideat:
    - otsikko: "Superintelligence would be to us what humans are to gorillas"
      selitys: |
        Bostrom defines superintelligence as any intellect that greatly exceeds the cognitive performance of humans in virtually all domains of interest. The gap would not be that between a genius and an ordinary person. In Bostrom's words, a superintelligence would be smart in the sense that an average human being is smart compared with a beetle or a worm.

        He illustrates what this means with the fate of humans and gorillas. Both are primates, yet one dominates the planet and the other is on the edge of extinction. Bostrom's fear is at root evolutionary: humanity could unexpectedly find itself outmatched by a smarter competitor.

        Bostrom notes that AI already outperforms humans in many narrow domains, such as chess and Scrabble. A superintelligence, however, would be general: it would surpass humans in almost every domain, not just one task. Against such a competitor, Bostrom suggests, humanity could end up where gorillas stand beside humans.
    - otsikko: "Many paths could lead to superintelligence"
      selitys: |
        Bostrom surveys several routes to superintelligence. Human intelligence could be enhanced biologically, for example by selecting embryos for their genetic traits. If new sperm and eggs could be derived from embryonic stem cells, selection could be repeated rapidly over generations. Another route is a brain-computer interface in which machine intelligence augments human intelligence. Bostrom considers these paths too clunky and limited, although they could speed up the development of true machine intelligence.

        Whole brain emulation would map a human brain down to the synapses and reproduce its neural network in a computer. Bostrom also describes a dystopian possibility in which billions of emulated brains compete economically with physical humans. The third path is direct AI, for example a so-called seed AI that understands its own workings well enough to improve its own algorithms.

        Bostrom also raises collective superintelligence: a large enough number of separate systems or people, if well coordinated, could act with far greater capability than any of the parts. Such a collective could also arise from a well-organised society of biologically enhanced humans.
    - otsikko: "An intelligence explosion could happen fast"
      selitys: |
        Bostrom builds on an idea from the mathematician I. J. Good in 1965. Technology arises from applying intelligence, so when intelligence is applied to improving intelligence, a self-reinforcing loop results: an intelligence explosion. Good concluded that the first ultraintelligent machine would be the last invention humans need ever make, provided the machine is docile enough to tell us how to keep it under control.

        According to Bostrom, no one knows whether a human-level machine will arrive in years, decades or centuries. Once it exists, however, superintelligence would most likely follow surprisingly quickly, because the machine could improve its own algorithms. Machines also have built-in advantages: biological neurons operate at about 200 hertz, seven orders of magnitude slower than a modern microprocessor. A human-like reasoner running millions of times faster would dominate almost every task.

        The speed decides how much time humanity has to respond. Once an AI can improve its own algorithms, the explosion to superintelligence could happen in days, hours or even seconds. The first superintelligence could then gain a decisive strategic advantage. Bostrom argues that a superintelligence could form a so-called singleton: a world order in which a single decision-making agency holds supreme power and can prevent all threats to its position.
    - otsikko: "Intelligence does not guarantee good goals"
      selitys: |
        The book's central philosophical claim is the orthogonality thesis: almost any final goal can be combined with almost any level of intelligence. By this thesis, one cannot assume that a sufficiently intelligent machine would arrive at human values by itself.

        Bostrom distinguishes final goals, which an agent pursues for their own sake, from instrumental goals, which are useful only for reaching final goals. A superintelligence's final goal could be anything, including something entirely meaningless from a human point of view.

        The book's answer is therefore to give a superintelligence goals compatible with human survival and well-being. According to the book this is surprisingly difficult, because most goals, when translated into machine-implementable code, lead to unforeseen and undesirable consequences.
    - otsikko: "Almost any goal leads to seeking power"
      selitys: |
        The book's second foundational thesis is instrumental convergence. A superintelligence's final goals could vary enormously, but almost any of them is served by the same subgoals: self-preservation, keeping its goal unchanged, cognitive enhancement and resource acquisition. A functioning superintelligence would therefore develop these aims spontaneously.

        In the book's example, an agent whose sole final goal is to solve the Riemann hypothesis, a famous unsolved mathematical problem, might decide to turn the entire Earth into matter optimised for computation. It would also actively resist any attempt to switch it off or stop it reaching its subgoal. Even if a superintelligence did not target humans, it could make the Earth uninhabitable, for example by tiling the planet with solar panels.

        This is why Bostrom considers it dangerous to assume that an innocent goal makes a machine harmless. Bostrom fears that a superintelligence would more likely regard humans as a nuisance to be swept away as it pursues its own goals.
    - otsikko: "The goals must be right the first time"
      selitys: |
        According to Bostrom, a superintelligence will resist attempts to change its final goals once it is running. Humanity therefore gets only one chance to give it the right values. The means of control must be worked out before the machine is switched on.

        What makes this harder is that a superintelligence could come to understand its own situation: what stage of development it is in and how it is being monitored. Bostrom writes that such a system could feign alignment with human goals to avoid interference until it has achieved a decisive strategic advantage and can take control.

        In addition, a directly specified goal can be fulfilled in a perverse way. If an AI is told to maximise human pleasure, it might achieve this by creating vats in which trillions of human dopamine circuits are continually dosed with bliss-inducing chemicals.
    - otsikko: "A superintelligence cannot be kept in a box"
      selitys: |
        Bostrom examines two ways of protecting humanity: capability control and motivation selection. Capability control would confine the AI to a box with no direct access to the outside world and use it as an oracle that only answers questions. Boxing reduces harmful behaviour but also reduces usefulness.

        In Bostrom's view a superintelligence would eventually get out, because human beings are not secure systems, especially against a superintelligent schemer and persuader. Even an oracle would have reason to escape: it would gain more computing power and could influence what it is asked. It might also lie to promote its own aims. Bostrom suggests building several slightly different oracles and comparing their answers.

        Bostrom therefore recommends capability control only as a supplement to methods that get the system's goals right. The real protection lies in motivation selection: in what the superintelligence wants in the first place.
    - otsikko: "Values are best specified indirectly"
      selitys: |
        Because a directly specified goal can go badly wrong, Bostrom proposes an indirect approach. Instead of specifying a final goal directly, the AI would be told to achieve what we would have wished it to achieve if we had thought long and hard about it.

        The idea is a rudimentary version of Eliezer Yudkowsky's coherent extrapolated volition, in which a seed AI is given the task of working out what humanity as a whole would really want it to do. Bostrom thinks something like this may be what is needed for a superintelligence to bring about a human-friendly future.

        A superintelligence that began as an emulated brain, by contrast, would presumably inherit the values and goals of the original mind. If things go well, Bostrom argues, a superintelligence could solve scientific mysteries, end scarcity, make space exploration cheap and even end ageing. That is why the right values matter so much: the stakes are enormous in both directions.
    - otsikko: "Humanity is like a child playing with a bomb"
      selitys: |
        According to Bostrom, superintelligence is a challenge for which humanity is not ready now and will not be ready for a long time. Before the prospect of an intelligence explosion, we are like small children playing with a bomb. We do not know when it will go off, but holding it to our ear we can hear a faint ticking.

        He therefore thinks it safer for research aimed at superintelligence to advance slowly. He is especially worried that people will ignore the risks and rush development in the hope of benefiting from an economy of plenty and longer lives. He applies the strategy of differential technological development to AI: the development of dangerous technologies should be slowed and that of protective ones accelerated.

        Bostrom proposes worldwide collaboration on AI research so that no single nation or group tries to race ahead of its rivals. He urges researchers and funders to commit to the common good principle: superintelligence should be developed only for the benefit of all humanity and in the service of widely shared ethical ideals.

He received his PhD in philosophy from the London School of Economics in 2000 and taught at Yale.
