  yleiskuva: |
    Jared Diamond lähtee liikkeelle kysymyksestä, jonka hänelle esitti Papua-Uuden-Guinean poliitikko Yali: miksi valkoiset kehittivät niin paljon tavaraa ja toivat sen Uuteen-Guineaan, mutta Uuden-Guinean asukkailla oli omaa tavaraa niin vähän? Diamond laajentaa kysymyksen koko maailmaan. Miksi Euraasiasta lähtöisin olevat kansat hallitsevat maailman vaurautta ja valtaa, ja miksi muiden mantereiden kansat tulivat valloitetuiksi eivätkä toisin päin?

    Kirjan mukaan vastaus ei ole perimässä eikä älykkyydessä. Diamond torjuu ajatuksen, että Euraasian valta-asema johtuisi älyllisestä, moraalisesta tai perinnöllisestä ylemmyydestä. Erot syntyivät ympäristöstä, ja niitä vahvistivat itseään ruokkivat kierteet. Ratkaisevaa oli, missä maanviljely pääsi alkamaan varhain ja leviämään laajalle.

    Kirja rakentaa selityksen ketjuna. Ensin tarvittiin villejä kasveja ja eläimiä, jotka soveltuivat kesytettäviksi. Niitä oli Euraasiassa, erityisesti Hedelmällisen puolikuun alueella, selvästi enemmän kuin muualla. Maanviljely tuotti ruoan ylijäämää, joka vapautti osan ihmisistä muihin töihin ja salli väestön kasvaa. Siitä seurasivat käsityöläiset, kirjurit, hallitsevat luokat, valtiot ja teknologia. Tiheä asutus ja kotieläinten läheisyys taas synnyttivät kulkutauteja, joille euraasialaiset kehittivät vähitellen vastustuskykyä. Kun eurooppalaiset kohtasivat Amerikan alkuperäisväestön, taudit tappoivat valtaosan siitä.

    Diamond yhdistää aineistossaan biologiaa, maantiedettä, antropologiaa ja historiaa. Johtopäätös on, että kansojen kohtalot selittyvät mantereiden eroilla, eivät kansojen omilla ominaisuuksilla. Kirja selittää myös, miksi juuri Länsi-Eurooppa eikä esimerkiksi Kiina nousi Euraasian sisällä valloittajaksi.

    Eurasian peoples did not come to dominate the world because they were smarter or more capable, but because their geography gave them better starting conditions for farming. Food surpluses, dense populations, diseases from livestock and an east–west continent added up over thousands of years to a head start that finally showed as guns, germs and steel.
  yleiskuva: |
    Jared Diamond starts from a question put to him by Yali, a politician from Papua New Guinea: why did white people develop so much cargo and bring it to New Guinea, while the people of New Guinea had so little cargo of their own? Diamond widens the question to the whole world. Why do peoples of Eurasian origin dominate the world's wealth and power, and why were the peoples of other continents conquered rather than the other way round?

    According to the book, the answer lies neither in genes nor in intelligence. Diamond rejects the idea that Eurasian dominance stems from any intellectual, moral or inherent genetic superiority. The differences came from the environment and were amplified by self-reinforcing loops. What mattered was where farming could start early and spread widely.

    The book builds its explanation as a chain. First came wild plants and animals suitable for domestication, which were far more plentiful in Eurasia, especially in the Fertile Crescent, than elsewhere. Farming produced food surpluses, which freed some people for other work and allowed populations to grow. From that followed craftsmen, scribes, ruling classes, states and technology. Dense settlement and closeness to livestock bred epidemic diseases, to which Eurasians gradually built up resistance. When Europeans met the native peoples of the Americas, those diseases killed most of them.

    Diamond combines biology, geography, anthropology and history. His conclusion is that the fates of peoples are explained by differences between continents, not by qualities of the peoples themselves. The book also explains why it was Western Europe, rather than China for example, that became the conqueror within Eurasia.
  ideat:
    - otsikko: "The differences come from the environment, not from peoples"
      selitys: |
        The book's basic claim is that gaps in power and technology between human groups originate mainly in environmental differences. Where cultural or genetic differences have favoured Eurasians, such as writing or resistance to disease, Diamond argues that these too arose from the influence of geography and were not inherent in Eurasian genes.

        Diamond explicitly rejects explanations that put Eurasian dominance down to intellectual, moral or genetic superiority. The documentary based on the book makes the same point: native hunter-gatherers were just as intelligent as people of European descent. Starting conditions and chance explain the differences, not the people themselves.
    - otsikko: "It all began with plants that could be domesticated"
      selitys: |
        The first step towards civilisation was the move from a mobile hunter-gatherer life to settled farming. According to the book, this required carbohydrate-rich plants that keep in storage, a climate dry enough for storage and animals suitable for domestication.

        Farming started early in the Fertile Crescent because it had plenty of wild wheat and pulses that were nutritious and easy to domesticate. Eurasia had barley, two kinds of wheat and three protein-rich pulses for food, and flax for textiles. American farmers, by contrast, had to struggle to develop maize from its probable wild ancestor, teosinte. The starting gaps were large before anyone had done anything.
    - otsikko: "Few animal species make good livestock"
      selitys: |
        Farming and transport needed large domestic animals. Diamond finds only 14 domesticated large mammal species worldwide out of 148 candidates, most of them from Eurasia. The five most useful, cattle, horses, sheep, goats and pigs, all descend from Eurasian species. South America had the llama, and the rest of the world practically nothing.

        Diamond explains this with the Anna Karenina principle: domestication succeeds only if every condition is met, and many promising species fail on a single shortcoming. An animal must, for example, be docile enough, live in herds, breed in captivity and have a social hierarchy. That is why Africa's zebras were never domesticated like their relative the horse. Many large animals also vanished from the Americas and Australia soon after people arrived there as already skilled hunters.
    - otsikko: "The direction of a continent decided what could spread"
      selitys: |
        Eurasia is large and stretches far from east to west. Diamond argues this mattered as much as the wealth of species. Along the same latitude, climate and seasons stay similar, so the same crops and animals could be raised from one end of the continent to the other. Innovations, languages and diseases spread with them.

        The Americas and Africa, by contrast, run north to south. Crops domesticated at one latitude did poorly at another, and Africa's climate varies so sharply from north to south that plants and animals could not reach areas where they might have thrived. According to the documentary based on the book, it was practically impossible for ideas, technology or even news to spread from north to south in the Americas.
    - otsikko: "Food surpluses created states and technology"
      selitys: |
        Once farming and herding produced a surplus, not everyone had to find food. The surplus allowed populations to grow and freed people to specialise, for example as craftsmen and scribes. Specialisation combined with population growth built up social and technical innovations that built on one another.

        Large societies developed ruling classes and the bureaucracies to support them, which grew into states and empires. Large domestic animals also provided power for ploughing and transport, which brought military and economic advantages. According to the book, civilisation did not come from special genius but from a sequence of developments, each made possible by the one before.
    - otsikko: "Livestock diseases did the conquerors' work"
      selitys: |
        Dense populations, busy trade and living close to livestock spread disease, including from animals to people. Smallpox, measles and influenza, Diamond says, arose from this closeness. Eurasians gradually built up resistance because these diseases circulated among them constantly.

        When Europeans reached the Americas, their diseases, to which the native population had no immunity, destroyed most of that population. By some estimates up to 95 percent of the native population of the Americas died after the conquest. The exchange did not work the other way, because the Americas' peoples had lived for a shorter time in dense communities close to domestic animals. As an exception, the book points to tropical diseases such as malaria, which limited European advances into Africa.
    - otsikko: "Cajamarca shows the whole chain at once"
      selitys: |
        The book's most famous scene is Cajamarca in 1532, where Francisco Pizarro's force met the Inca ruler Atahualpa. According to the documentary based on the book, 168 Spanish conquistadors defeated an Inca army of about 80,000.

        Diamond uses the episode to show how long-term factors came together in a single moment. The Spaniards had steel weapons, horses and writing. Atahualpa had never seen a book, and writing had never reached Peru. The book's title refers to exactly these means, guns, germs and steel, by which farming societies conquered others even when they were vastly outnumbered.
    - otsikko: "Europe's fragmentation beat China's unity"
      selitys: |
        Diamond also asks why Western Europe rather than China became the conqueror. Advanced civilisation arose first in places like China, India and Mesopotamia, whose geography lacked natural barriers. Large empires ruled there for thousands of years.

        Europe's mountains, rivers and coastlines split it into smaller, competing states. At a later stage this became an advantage. A unified, isolated empire could keep up a mistaken policy for a long time without immediate consequences, as China did when it gave up its naval mastery by banning ocean-going ships. In Europe, competition between neighbours kept governments from suppressing progress for long: if the Pope banned Galileo's work, it could be reprinted in Protestant countries.

    Jared Diamond (born 1937) is an American scientist and author. Originally trained in biochemistry and physiology, he was a professor of physiology at the UCLA School of Medicine and later a professor of geography at UCLA until 2024.
