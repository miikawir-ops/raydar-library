    James Clear kysyy, miten hyviä tottumuksia rakennetaan ja huonoja puretaan niin, että muutos pysyy. Kirjan lähtökohta on, että tottumukset ovat itsensä kehittämisen korkoa korolle. Kun parantaa toimintaansa joka päivä prosentin, tulos on vuoden päästä moninkertainen, vaikka yksittäinen parannus tuskin näkyy.

    Kirja neuvoo korvaamaan haitallisen tottumuksen, kuten vitkuttelun tai riippuvuuden, pienellä ja vähän ponnistelua vaativalla myönteisellä tottumuksella. Kun tällaiset pienet tottumukset muuttuvat rutiiniksi, niiden varaan voi rakentaa suurempia, joihin tahdonvoima ei muuten riittäisi.

    Kirjan mukaan ongelma ei ole ihmisessä vaan hänen järjestelmässään: verkostossa, jonka hän on itse aiemmin rakentanut ja joka nyt estää muutoksen. Huonot tottumukset toistuvat, koska muutoksen järjestelmä on väärä, ei siksi, ettei ihminen haluaisi muuttua. Samalla tavalla järjestelmä voi estää hyviä tottumuksia, mutta sen voi purkaa vähitellen. Clear nojaa biologian, psykologian ja neurotieteen tutkimukseen ja tarinoihin olympiavoittajista, taiteilijoista, yritysjohtajista, lääkäreistä ja koomikoista.

    Kirja etenee kolmesta perusajatuksesta käytännön keinoihin. Pienet parannukset kasautuvat. Järjestelmät ovat tärkeämpiä kuin tavoitteet. Pysyvä muutos alkaa identiteetistä, eli siitä, millaiseksi ihmiseksi haluaa tulla. Sen jälkeen Clear purkaa tottumuksen neljään vaiheeseen: vihjeeseen, himoon, reaktioon ja palkkioon. Niistä hän johtaa neljä käyttäytymisen muutoksen lakia: tee hyvästä tottumuksesta ilmeinen, houkutteleva, helppo ja tyydyttävä. Huonon tottumuksen purkamiseen lait käännetään.

    Johtopäätös on, että pysyvä muutos ei vaadi radikaalia muutosta. Pienet, toistuvat teot muuttavat sekä tuloksia että käsitystä itsestä. Kirjasta tuli valtava menestys: sitä on myyty kymmeniä miljoonia kappaleita, ja se oli vuosia New York Timesin bestseller-listalla.

Sen sijaan, että uusi tottumus sidotaan tiettyyn aikaan ja paikkaan, se sidotaan olemassa olevaan tottumukseen, joka toimii selvänä vihjeenä.     Big changes come from small habits that compound over time like interest. So focus on systems rather than goals, build habits around a new identity, and make good habits obvious, attractive, easy and satisfying.
  yleiskuva: |
    James Clear asks how to build good habits and break bad ones so that the change lasts. The book's starting point is that habits are the compound interest of self-improvement. Getting 1 per cent better every day adds up to a many-fold result after a year, even though each single improvement is barely visible.

    The book advises replacing a harmful habit, such as procrastination or an addiction, with a small, low-effort positive habit. Once such small habits become routine, they support larger ones that willpower alone would not sustain.

    According to the book, the problem is not the person but their system: a network of barriers they have built themselves that now blocks change. Bad habits repeat not because people do not want to change but because they have the wrong system for change. The same system can hold back good habits, but it can be taken apart over time. Clear draws on research in biology, psychology and neuroscience and on stories of Olympic gold medallists, artists, business leaders, physicians and comedians.

    The book moves from three core ideas to practical tools. Small improvements compound. Systems matter more than goals. Lasting change starts with identity: the kind of person you want to become. Clear then breaks a habit into four stages: cue, craving, response and reward. From them he derives the Four Laws of Behaviour Change: make a good habit obvious, attractive, easy and satisfying. To break a bad habit, the laws are inverted.

    The conclusion is that lasting change does not require radical change. Small, repeated actions change both results and how people see themselves. The book became a huge success: it has sold tens of millions of copies and spent years on the New York Times bestseller list.
  ideat:
    - otsikko: "Small improvements compound into big ones"
      selitys: |
        The book argues that people overestimate the importance of one defining moment and underestimate the value of small daily improvements. We readily assume that big success needs big action, whether it is losing weight, building a business or writing a book.

        Clear illustrates the alternative with arithmetic. If you get 1 per cent better each day for a year, you end up about 37 times better. If you get 1 per cent worse each day, you decline nearly to zero. A 1 per cent improvement is not particularly notable and may not even be noticeable, but in the long run it is meaningful. So, according to the book, it does not matter how successful someone is now, but whether their habits are putting them on the path to success. Habits are the compound interest of self-improvement.
    - otsikko: "British Cycling won through tiny improvements"
      selitys: |
        The book's best-known example is British Cycling. British riders had endured nearly a hundred years of mediocrity: since 1908 they had won just one Olympic gold medal, and no British cyclist had ever won the Tour de France. The new performance director, Dave Brailsford, adopted a strategy he called the aggregation of marginal gains: improve everything that goes into riding a bike by 1 per cent.

        His coaches redesigned the bike seats for comfort, tested clothing in a wind tunnel and worked out the best pillow and mattress for each rider. They hired a surgeon to teach riders how to wash their hands to avoid colds and painted the inside of the team truck white to spot dust. Five years later, at the 2008 Beijing Olympics, the British won 60 per cent of the road and track cycling gold medals. From 2007 to 2017, British cyclists won 178 world championships, 66 Olympic or Paralympic gold medals and five Tours de France.
    - otsikko: "Forget goals and focus on your system"
      selitys: |
        According to the book, goals are about the results you want to achieve, while systems are the processes that lead to those results. In Clear's words, you do not rise to the level of your goals; you fall to the level of your systems.

        So difficulty in changing habits is not a personal failing. Bad habits repeat because the system for change is wrong, not because people do not want to change. The purpose of goals is to win the game; the purpose of systems is to keep playing it. The book describes true long-term thinking as goal-less thinking: it is not about any single achievement but about a cycle of endless refinement, in which commitment to the process determines progress.
    - otsikko: "Lasting change starts with identity"
      selitys: |
        The book argues that the deepest layer of behaviour change is identity. Current behaviour reflects the kind of person you believe you are, consciously or not. To change behaviour for good, you have to start believing new things about yourself. Clear calls these identity-based habits.

        In his words, the goal is not to run a marathon but to become a runner. Changing identity is a two-step process: decide the type of person you want to be, then prove it to yourself with small wins. Every action is a vote for the type of person you wish to become. No single action changes your beliefs, but as the votes build up, so does the evidence of a new identity. That is why meaningful change does not require radical change.
    - otsikko: "A habit is a four-step loop"
      selitys: |
        Clear breaks every habit into four steps: cue, craving, response and reward. The cue triggers a craving, which motivates a response. The response provides a reward, which satisfies the craving and becomes associated with the cue. Together the steps form a neurological feedback loop, the habit loop, which makes behaviour automatic.

        The book's example is turning on a light. In a dark room a person gets the cue that they need light. They crave better vision and switch on the light, and the reward is being able to see clearly. Breaking a habit into its parts shows how it works and where it can be changed. This breakdown is the basis of the book's most practical part, the Four Laws of Behaviour Change.
    - otsikko: "Four laws: obvious, attractive, easy and satisfying"
      selitys: |
        From the four steps of the habit loop, Clear derives the Four Laws of Behaviour Change. To build a good habit, make it obvious (cue), attractive (craving), easy (response) and satisfying (reward). At each step you can ask how to make the habit so.

        To break a bad habit, the laws are inverted: make it invisible, unattractive, difficult and unsatisfying. According to the book, designing your environment is essential here. The cues for good habits are put in view and the cues for bad ones out of sight. The purpose of the laws is to make good habits inevitable and bad habits impossible.
    - otsikko: "Make it obvious: attach a new habit to an old one"
      selitys: |
        One of the book's tools is habit stacking. You identify a habit you already do each day and stack the new behaviour on top of it. The formula is: after I do my current habit, I will do the new one. For example: after I pour my morning coffee, I will meditate for one minute; or after I take off my work shoes, I will change straight into my workout clothes.

        Clear describes habit stacking as a special form of implementation intention. Rather than tying the new habit to a particular time and place, you tie it to an existing habit, which serves as a clear cue. The method was created by BJ Fogg as part of his Tiny Habits programme. The book also presents temptation bundling, in which you give yourself a small reward for doing the stacked habits.
    - otsikko: "Make it easy: the two-minute rule"
      selitys: |
        The two-minute rule says that when you start a new habit, it should take less than two minutes to do. Nearly any habit can be scaled down to a two-minute version. Read before bed each night becomes read one page. Do thirty minutes of yoga becomes take out my yoga mat. Fold the laundry becomes fold one pair of socks.

        The point is to make starting so easy that you will do it. It ties to the book's core idea: once small, low-effort habits become routine, they gradually support larger ones that willpower alone would not sustain. Clear also notes that good habits come more naturally to some people than to others. People with higher oxytocin levels, for example, tend to be more agreeable and may be more inclined to habits such as writing thank-you notes.
