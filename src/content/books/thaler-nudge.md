    People make predictable mistakes, and the way choices are presented always shapes what they choose. So decisions can be steered towards better outcomes with small, cheap nudges, such as good default options, without taking away anyone's freedom to choose otherwise.
  yleiskuva: |
    Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein ask how people could be helped to make better decisions about health, wealth and happiness without losing their freedom of choice. The book draws on research in psychology and behavioural economics.

    Its starting point is a critique of the textbook economist's view of human beings. In the textbooks, people (homo economicus) always think and choose without error. The authors argue that real people make predictable mistakes, because they use rules of thumb, fall into fallacies and are influenced by others. The book describes human thinking as two systems, a fast automatic one and a reflective one, and works through the most common biases with examples.

    This leads to the book's key concept, choice architecture: the way choices are presented, which influences what people choose. The authors defend libertarian paternalism: it is legitimate for choice architects to steer people towards longer, healthier and better lives, as long as everyone remains free to choose otherwise. The tool is the nudge, a change in the choice environment that alters behaviour in a predictable way without forbidding options or significantly changing economic incentives.

    The rest of the book applies the idea to policy. It makes proposals for finance, health, the environment, schools and marriage: for example automatic retirement saving, the Save More Tomorrow programme, reform of Medicare's drug benefit and mandated choice for organ donation. The conclusion is that many social problems can be at least partly addressed by improving choice architecture. A revised edition appeared in 2021 as Nudge: The Final Edition.
  ideat:
    - otsikko: "People are not the infallible actors of economics"
      selitys: |
        The book challenges economics' traditional view of human beings. The textbook homo economicus always thinks and chooses well. Thaler and Sunstein cite a body of research that raises serious questions about how rational people's judgements and decisions really are.

        According to the authors, real people make mistakes, and those mistakes are predictable. They come from rules of thumb, fallacies and the way other people influence us. This view of human nature is the book's main justification for steering choices.
    - otsikko: "Thinking has an automatic and a reflective system"
      selitys: |
        The book describes human thinking as two systems. The Automatic System is fast and instinctive and does not involve what we usually call thinking. It is at work when we smile at a puppy, get nervous in air turbulence or duck when a ball is thrown at us. The Reflective System is slow and self-conscious. It is at work when we choose a college, a holiday destination or whether to marry.

        According to the book, the differences and conflicts between the two systems are why people make recurring mistakes. The same distinction is set out more fully in Daniel Kahneman's Thinking, Fast and Slow.
    - otsikko: "Rules of thumb lead to systematic biases"
      selitys: |
        The book works through the most common biases. In anchoring, people rely too heavily on a single starting point. Asked to guess the population of Milwaukee, a Chicago resident starts from Chicago's roughly three million and divides it, while a resident of Green Bay starts from their own city's roughly 100,000 and multiplies it. The guesses differ widely just because of the starting point. In the availability heuristic, people judge how common something is by how easily an example comes to mind. That is why people think homicides are more common than suicides.

        In representativeness, people see meaningful patterns in random data, such as imagined cancer clusters or the hot hand in basketball. The book argues that these biases also affect business and politics: people overstate risks, buy unnecessary insurance, and governments pursue the wrong goals.
    - otsikko: "We stick with the status quo and follow others"
      selitys: |
        Two tendencies matter especially for the book. The first is status quo bias: people keep to a familiar course of action even when it is clearly not in their interest. In the book's example, a magazine offers a free trial after which the subscription continues and is charged until the customer actively cancels it. Many end up paying for magazines they do not read.

        The second is social influence. People are strongly swayed by what others do. The authors cite Solomon Asch's famous experiment, in which peer pressure led people to give clearly wrong answers, such as saying that two lines of different lengths were the same.
    - otsikko: "Choice architecture always shapes choices"
      selitys: |
        Choice architecture is the way choices are presented, and the book argues that presentation influences decisions. People can be nudged by arranging the choice environment in a certain way without taking away their freedom of choice.

        The book's simple example is a school cafeteria. When healthy food is placed at eye level and less healthy food is harder to reach, pupils eat less junk food and more healthy food. Nobody is forbidden to eat anything.

        Thaler wanted the subtitle to be The Gentle Power of Choice Architecture, because in his words that is the book's message: people are on the receiving end of choice architecture in almost every part of their lives, and understanding that helps. The book's basic rule is to make the desired behaviour easy. To encourage something, find out why people are not already doing it and remove the barriers. The corollary is that adding barriers makes behaviour harder.
    - otsikko: "Libertarian paternalism combines freedom and guidance"
      selitys: |
        The book defends an approach that combines two political ideas usually seen as opposites. The libertarian part means that people should generally be free to do what they like and to opt out of arrangements they do not want. The paternalistic part means that it is legitimate for choice architects to try to influence people's behaviour so that their lives are longer, healthier and better.

        The authors' main justification rests on human nature and psychology: people make predictable mistakes. The term arose when Thaler presented results at the University of Chicago in which people's saving rates had tripled in three years without anyone being forced. His colleague Casey Mulligan asked whether this was not paternalism. Thaler replied that if it was, it was a new kind, and suggested the name libertarian paternalism. The authors developed the idea in articles in 2003 before the book.
    - otsikko: "A nudge is cheap, easy to avoid and leaves incentives unchanged"
      selitys: |
        According to the book, a nudge is any aspect of choice architecture that changes people's behaviour in a predictable way without forbidding options or significantly changing their economic incentives. To count as a mere nudge, an intervention must be easy and cheap to avoid. Nudges are not mandates.

        Nudges are not taxes, fines, subsidies, bans or mandates. Putting fruit at eye level is a nudge; banning junk food is not. The good choice is made easier, while the bad one remains possible.
    - otsikko: "Defaults and advance commitment help people save"
      selitys: |
        The book applies these ideas to retirement saving. The authors argue that many Americans do not save enough for retirement, and that in 2005 the personal savings rate was negative for the first time since the Great Depression years of 1932 and 1933.

        The first proposal is a better default. Employees can choose any retirement plan, but if they do nothing they are automatically enrolled in an expertly designed programme. The second is the Save More Tomorrow programme. It tackles the problem of people who want to save more but keep putting it off. Participants commit in advance to a series of contribution increases timed to coincide with pay rises. Thaler created the programme with Shlomo Benartzi. It uses the fact that people have more self-control about the future, and ties the increases to pay rises because people feel losses more strongly than gains, so take-home pay never falls. It later turned out that what really mattered was making the increases automatic and getting people signed up.
    - otsikko: "Good choice architecture improves health and public services"
      selitys: |
        The book analyses the Bush administration's Medicare Part D drug-benefit programme. The authors find it on the right track in some respects but cumbersome as choice architecture. Default choices were assigned at random, and older people were not given adequate means to choose among a huge number of options. The book proposes that people who do not sign up should be assigned a suitable plan and that everyone should receive a yearly itemised list of the drugs they used and the fees they paid, so that plans are easy to compare.

        To raise organ donation, the book proposes mandated choice: when renewing a driving licence, everyone must state whether they want to be an organ donor. The authors also recommend websites showing that the community supports donation, so that people are nudged into becoming donors by others' example. In the revised edition they explain why presumed consent is less effective than it looks. In Austria, for example, hardly anyone opts out, but failing to opt out says little about a person's real wishes, and on its own it does not decide whether organs are used.
    - otsikko: "The revised edition: sludge and climate"
      selitys: |
        The Final Edition of 2021 updates the book. A new concept is sludge, the opposite of a nudge. Where a good nudge makes things easy, sludge makes them hard: huge forms, complicated applications and business practices that get in the way. The authors note that the US government imposes some 11 billion hours of paperwork a year, and that sludge often works as a wall that keeps people from permits, benefits or health care they are entitled to.

        The chapter on climate change was heavily revised. As Thaler puts it, climate change cannot be solved with nudging, but it cannot be solved without nudging either. Prices have to be set right first, for example through a carbon tax, and only then can nudges and technology help people act on them. The book also proposes smart disclosure, under which every feature of a mortgage, for example, would be available online in a comparable form.
