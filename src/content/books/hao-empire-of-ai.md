  yleiskuva: |
    Karen Hao kertoo OpenAI:n tarinan, mutta kirjan mukaan se ei ole yrityskirja. Yhtiön historia on prisma, jonka kautta Hao tarkastelee laajempaa ilmiötä: miten tieteellinen kunnianhimo muuttui aggressiiviseksi, ideologiseksi ja rahan ohjaamaksi kilpajuoksuksi ja miten sen vaikutukset ulottuvat ympäri maailmaa. Kirja on samalla pohdinta vallasta.

    Hao aloitti OpenAI:n seuraamisen MIT Technology Review'n toimittajana vuonna 2019 ja vietti aikaa yhtiön toimistoilla. OpenAI oli perustettu voittoa tavoittelemattomaksi tutkimusorganisaatioksi, jonka piti kehittää tekoälyä koko ihmiskunnan hyödyksi ja jakaa tutkimuksensa avoimesti. Hao huomasi kuitenkin, että yhtiö oli hyvin salamyhkäinen ja kilpailuhenkinen: sen johto halusi ehdottomasti olla ensimmäinen, joka saavuttaa yleisen tekoälyn (AGI, artificial general intelligence). Ristiriita avoimuuden ja kilpailun välillä on kirjan lähtökohta.

    Aineistona on noin 300 haastattelua noin 260 ihmisen kanssa sekä kirjeenvaihtoa, Slack-keskusteluja ja asiakirjoja. OpenAI ei osallistunut kirjan tekoon, eikä Sam Altman antanut haastattelua. Kirja kulkee kahdella tasolla. Se kuvaa sisältäpäin OpenAI:n päätökset, ChatGPT:n synnyn ja Altmanin erottamisen ja paluun vuonna 2023. Lisäksi se raportoi paikoista, joissa tekoälyn kustannukset näkyvät: Kenian, Kolumbian, Filippiinien ja Venezuelan datatyöläisistä sekä Chilen yhteisöistä, jotka vastustavat datakeskuksia.

    Kirjan keskeinen väite on, että tekoäly-yhtiöt toimivat kuin 1800-luvun siirtomaaimperiumit. Pieni joukko ihmisiä tekee päätöksiä kaikkien muiden puolesta, ottaa haltuunsa resursseja, jotka eivät ole sen omia, ja käyttää hyväksi halpaa työvoimaa. Samalla imperiumit perustelevat toimintaansa sivistystehtävällä ja sillä, että juuri niiden on voitettava kilpailevat, pahemmat imperiumit. Ne eivät ole yhtä avoimen väkivaltaisia kuin vanhat imperiumit, mutta logiikka on sama.

    Johtopäätös on, että nykyinen tapa kehittää tekoälyä paisuttamalla malleja, dataa ja laskentakapasiteettia on ideologinen valinta eikä luonnonlaki. Kirjan epilogi kuvaa, miten imperiumi voi kaatua, ja kirja korostaa, että avoimempia ja vähemmän resursseja kuluttavia vaihtoehtoja on olemassa.

  ydin: |
    The AI industry has become a new kind of empire. A few companies, led by OpenAI, take hold of data, labour, land, energy and water, and a small group of people makes decisions that affect the whole world. This path is not inevitable: it follows from ideological choices that can be challenged.
  yleiskuva: |
    Karen Hao tells the story of OpenAI, but the book describes itself as not a corporate book. The company's history is a prism for a wider phenomenon: how a scientific ambition turned into an aggressive, ideological, money-fuelled race, and how its effects reach around the world. The book is also a meditation on power.

    Hao began covering OpenAI for MIT Technology Review in 2019 and spent time in its offices. OpenAI had been founded as a non-profit research organisation that would develop AI for the benefit of all humanity and share its research openly. Yet Hao found the company secretive and fiercely competitive: its leaders were set on being first to artificial general intelligence (AGI). That tension between openness and competition is the book's starting point.

    The book draws on about 300 interviews with around 260 people, along with correspondence, Slack conversations and documents. OpenAI declined to cooperate, and Sam Altman did not give an interview. The book works on two levels. It follows OpenAI's decisions from the inside, the creation of ChatGPT and Altman's firing and return in 2023. It also reports from the places where the costs of AI show: data workers in Kenya, Colombia, the Philippines and Venezuela, and communities in Chile resisting data centres.

    Its central claim is that AI companies behave like the colonial empires of the 1800s. A small group decides for everyone else, takes resources that are not its own and exploits cheap labour. The empires justify themselves with a civilising mission and with the need to beat rival, worse empires. They are not as openly violent as the old empires, but the logic is the same.

    The book concludes that the current way of building AI, by endlessly scaling up models, data and computing, is an ideological choice, not a law of nature. Its epilogue describes how the empire could fall, and the book stresses that more open and less resource-hungry alternatives exist.
  ideat:
    - otsikko: "The AI industry is a new kind of empire"
      selitys: |
        Hao uses empire as the lens through which she understands the whole AI industry. In the old empires, a small group made decisions whose consequences everyone else had to bear without a say. The book argues that the same arrangement is returning: a few companies decide what data they may take, who gets privacy and who gets economic opportunity.

        The features of empire recur throughout. The companies seize resources that are not theirs: the work of artists and writers, the text people have posted online, and the land, energy and water that data centres need. They exploit labour around the world. And they justify all this with a civilising mission, the promise that AI will benefit everyone. The difference from the old empires is that the exploitation is less openly violent. Hao warns that democracy cannot survive in a world where most people have lost agency over their own lives.
    - otsikko: "The promise of openness became a race"
      selitys: |
        OpenAI was founded as a counterweight to the big technology companies. It was a non-profit, its research was meant to be open, and its mission was to develop AI for the benefit of all humanity. According to the book, the company then reasoned its way out of its own founding idea. To develop AI ethically, it had to be ahead of everyone else. To be ahead, it needed enormous investment. And to attract that investment, the non-profit gained a for-profit structure alongside it and billions from Microsoft.

        Hao stresses that this was not just a clash of personalities, although there were plenty, including Elon Musk's departure. Underneath lay an ideological conflict. The wish to be first and the wish to be open were in tension from the start, and competition won.
    - otsikko: "OpenAI turned AGI into an arms race for resources"
      selitys: |
        The book argues that OpenAI was the first to turn the pursuit of artificial general intelligence into a race won by whoever has the most resources. DeepMind had also talked about AGI, but it did not treat it as a race. OpenAI cast itself as the anti-Google and set out to scale up its models, its training data, and its data centres and supercomputers aggressively.

        That set off an industry-wide race joined by established companies such as Google and Meta and by new ones such as Anthropic. In Hao's account, this constant escalation of scale and resource use is what is pushing us towards catastrophe. Speaking about the book, Hao has stressed that today's generative AI is only one narrow way of building AI. It became dominant because it suited the companies' ideology and business, not because it is the only possible path.
    - otsikko: "Quasi-religious beliefs drive the work"
      selitys: |
        According to Hao, AI development is deeply ideological. Two quasi-religious camps shape the field. Some believe AI could lead the world to utopia and want to race towards it as fast as possible. Others believe AI could destroy humanity, yet want to build it first so that a less responsible actor does not get there before them.

        Both camps arrive at the same conclusion: hurry. The book shows how this logic played out inside OpenAI. Its chief scientist Ilya Sutskever, for example, had become obsessed with AGI by summer 2023, while growing worried about how the company was handling the technology it was building. The book argues that this religious framing also serves as the empire's justification: only we know how to save humanity.
    - otsikko: "Sam Altman is a storyteller and fundraiser"
      selitys: |
        Altman is neither an inventor nor an engineer. Hao describes his particular skill as fundraising, built on an exceptional talent for telling persuasive stories about the future. He understands what each person wants to hear and paints a vision they want to commit their money or authority to.

        From her reporting, Hao concludes that what Altman says is more closely tied to what he thinks his listener needs to hear than to what he believes or to the facts. The book traces how different people gradually come to feel they are being manipulated. This comes to a head in the book's most dramatic episode, when OpenAI's board fired Altman in 2023 and he soon returned. Hao tells the full story of what happened.
    - otsikko: "AI's clean surface rests on cheap, punishing labour"
      selitys: |
        The book describes people whose work makes AI usable but stays hidden from its users. When OpenAI began building a consumer product, it needed a filter to stop its model producing violent and sexual content. The work was outsourced to the firm Sama in Kenya.

        Day after day, Kenyan workers read some of the most horrific text on the internet, along with variations generated by AI itself, and categorised it in detail: was it violence or sexual abuse, and did it involve children. The work broke the workers and the people close to them. Hao contrasts this with how the companies value their staff: researchers receive pay packages worth millions, while the companies treat content labelling as work worth about two dollars an hour. For Hao the division is ideological. It exposes the empire's hierarchy, in which some people's humanity counts for less.
    - otsikko: "Data centres take land, energy and water"
      selitys: |
        AI built on scaling needs enormous physical infrastructure. The hardware requires minerals such as copper and lithium, and cooling data centres and generating their electricity takes fresh, even drinkable, water. The book argues that data centres are often placed in communities with little economic or political power to resist them or even to learn about them.

        Its example is Cerrillos in Chile. Residents there spent years opposing a proposed Google data centre that, in the middle of a drought, would have drawn on one of the few public water sources in a country where water had been privatised under the dictatorship. By the corrected calculation, the data centre could use as much water in a year as all of Cerrillos's roughly 88,000 residents. Just getting the company's attention took the residents an enormous effort.
    - otsikko: "The empire is not inevitable"
      selitys: |
        The book's epilogue turns to how the empire could fall. Hao argues that today's AI is not the natural course of the technology but a choice made by a few people on the basis of their beliefs and business interests. That is exactly why it can be changed.

        Hao argues that public attention should shift from speculation about AI sentience to its present effects on work and the environment. The book points out that more open and less energy-intensive ways of building AI exist. Drawing on the book, Hao has proposed ways to take back control: stronger data privacy and transparency rules, updated intellectual property protection, labour standards and minimum wages for data workers, and support for more diverse AI research.

    Karen Hao (born around 1993) is an American journalist who specialises in artificial intelligence.
